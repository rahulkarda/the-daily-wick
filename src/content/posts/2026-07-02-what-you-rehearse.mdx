## The Idea

Every time you check your phone out of habit rather than need, you rehearse distraction. Every time you scroll instead of sitting with boredom, you practice being incapable of boredom. Every time you open a new tab because the current task feels difficult, you train yourself to flee difficulty.

None of this looks like practice. It looks like nothing — a small reflex, a moment that disappears. But attention patterns are built exactly the way physical skills are: through repetition. The nervous system doesn't distinguish between a deliberate training session and a thoughtless reflex. Both are reps. Both shape the pattern.

This means the question to ask about any repeated behavior is not only "is this harmful right now" but "what am I practicing by doing this?" Scrolling for five minutes isn't a problem. Scrolling-as-default-response-to-any-uncomfortable-pause is a curriculum. You're taking a course in not being able to wait.

The same logic runs in reverse. Every time you sit with discomfort for a few minutes instead of reaching for your phone, you rehearse tolerance. Every time you finish a hard thing before switching, you practice completion. The mind is always in training. The only question is what it's training for.

## One Question

What is the repeated behavior you most wish you could stop — and what exactly are you rehearsing each time you do it?

## Today's Action

Choose one small, habitual distraction you reach for when things get uncomfortable. For the next 24 hours, when you feel the urge, pause for 60 seconds before acting on it. Don't suppress the urge — just delay it by one minute.

Notice what the pause feels like. That sensation is the thing you've been rehearsing away.

## Go Deeper

You can't simply stop rehearsing the old pattern. You have to replace it with a different rehearsal. The question is not willpower but substitution: what do you want to practice instead?
