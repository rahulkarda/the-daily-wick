## The Idea

The calendar shows what you said you'd do. Your actual attention — where you look, what you finish, what you linger on — shows what you care about.

Most people have a significant gap between the two. The morning exercise block, the deep-work slot, the "review project goals" reminder: these are aspirational placeholders. They represent the person you intended to be this week. Your phone's screen-time data, your browser history, and the actual state of your desk tell a different story — one about what you actually chose, minute by minute, when choosing felt frictionless.

This is not a moral failure. It's information. The calendar is a statement of values at the moment of planning, when intention is high and competing demands feel abstract. Attention is a statement of values in real time, when intention meets friction. The gap between them is the exact size of the work you still have to do — not productivity work, but the more fundamental work of understanding what you actually want.

The calendar cannot close that gap by itself. Blocking more hours doesn't change what happens inside them. The more useful question is not "what should I schedule" but "what does my actual attention, honestly tracked, tell me I value right now" — and whether you're comfortable with that answer.

## One Question

If someone looked at your phone usage and calendar side by side this week, what would they conclude you care most about?

## Today's Action

Pull up your screen-time data or browser history from yesterday. Don't judge it — just read it as data. Write down three things it says you valued. Then write down three things your calendar says you valued. Note the gap.

That gap is the starting point for honest planning.

## Go Deeper

The calendar lie is not about dishonesty. It's about the distance between who we are in a planning moment and who we are when the moment actually comes. Closing that distance requires looking at both with equal seriousness.
