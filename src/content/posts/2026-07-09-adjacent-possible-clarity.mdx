## The Question

We are constantly encouraged to envision our 'five-year plan,' to define our 'north star,' to gaze fixedly at a distant, ideal future. This pursuit of ultimate clarity—a perfectly detailed vision of where we're headed—is often framed as the prerequisite for meaningful action. Without a grand vision, how can we possibly take the right steps? How can we ensure alignment and avoid wasting effort on divergent paths? The tension lies in whether this far-sighted clarity is genuinely helpful or, paradoxically, a source of paralysis. Some argue that without a clear destination, we drift aimlessly. Others suggest that an overly rigid, distant vision blinds us to emergent opportunities and the invaluable feedback that comes from simply taking the next reasonable step. Is the goal to see the entire path, or just enough of the path to move forward intelligently? The modern world often demands both, yet the two approaches feel inherently at odds. We want the certainty of a fixed point, but life rarely offers it. How do we reconcile the desire for grand vision with the reality of incremental progress and uncertain terrain?

## The Argument

The concept of the "adjacent possible," popularized by Steven Johnson, offers a powerful alternative to the demand for ultimate clarity. It suggests that innovation and progress don't leap across vast conceptual chasms but rather emerge from the immediate, next-door possibilities. Think of a room full of doors: you can't see what's behind the third door until you've opened the first, stepped through, and discovered the new set of adjacent doors it reveals. This isn't just a metaphor for scientific discovery; it's a profound model for personal and professional clarity.

Our minds, much like biological systems, operate most effectively when presented with manageable, proximate challenges. Attempting to map out every single detail of a distant future can be overwhelming, leading to analysis paralysis. Instead, by focusing on the adjacent possible, we cultivate a dynamic form of clarity: the clarity of the *next solvable problem*. This approach leverages our inherent capacity for learning and adaptation. Each step taken, each door opened, provides new information that refines our understanding and reveals new, previously unseen possibilities. This is how genuine breakthroughs often occur, not through a perfectly pre-ordained plan, but through an iterative process of exploration and discovery. As Nobel laureate Herbert Simon noted, humans are not boundless optimizers but "satisficers" – we seek solutions that are "good enough" and move on, allowing new information to shape subsequent choices. This iterative process, guided by the adjacent possible, often leads to more robust and adaptable outcomes than rigid adherence to a grand, distant plan.

## The Counterpoint

A common and legitimate counter-argument to the adjacent possible is that without a clear, long-term vision, one risks meandering without purpose. If you only ever look to the next step, how do you know you're headed in the right direction? Critics might argue that this approach encourages short-sightedness, an endless pursuit of novelty without a unifying theme. A business without a five-year strategy might chase every emerging trend, losing its core identity. An individual without a defined career goal might jump from job to job, never building deep expertise or achieving significant impact. This perspective suggests that while the adjacent possible might be good for tactical improvisation, it lacks the strategic depth required for meaningful, sustained progress. It risks confusing activity with direction, and getting lost in the immediate without ever arriving anywhere truly significant. The fear is that without a lighthouse on the distant horizon, we might just keep sailing in circles, albeit with fascinating detours along the way.

However, this objection often misinterprets the adjacent possible as an absence of *any* direction. It’s not about aimless wandering; it’s about having a flexible compass rather than a rigid map. What survives the objection is the understanding that while a general sense of direction (e.g., "I want to contribute to sustainable energy" or "I want to write a book") is valuable, the *specificity* of the distant future is often unknowable and counterproductive. We concede that some overarching intention is useful, but we push back against the demand for granular, fixed clarity over a long timeline. The real pressure point is not *whether* to have a vision, but *how detailed* that vision needs to be, and how much it should dictate current action versus allowing for emergent pathways.

## What To Do With It

Instead of stressing over a perfectly articulated, distant future, commit to clarifying your *next adjacent move*. This isn't about setting smaller goals; it's about shifting your mindset from exhaustive planning to intelligent exploration. For the next week, identify one area where you feel stuck trying to see the whole picture. Instead, ask yourself: "Given what I know right now, what is the single most reasonable, smallest next step I can take?" This step should be concrete, achievable, and designed to generate new information. It might be a conversation, a small experiment, or a specific piece of research. The goal is not to reach the destination, but to open the next door. Trust that the view from the other side will offer new, clearer choices than you could possibly imagine from your current vantage point. The clarity you seek often reveals itself in motion, not in static contemplation.

*What is the next door you need to open, not the whole mansion you need to blueprint?*
