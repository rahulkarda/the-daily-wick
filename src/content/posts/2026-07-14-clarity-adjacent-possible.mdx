## The Question

We are constantly exhorted to 'find our clarity,' to articulate a grand vision, a singular purpose that will guide all our actions. The advice often comes with the implicit promise that once this overarching clarity is achieved, all subsequent decisions will fall neatly into place. But what if this pursuit of a fixed, distant clarity is, paradoxically, a source of confusion and paralysis? What if the very act of trying to define a perfect, unmoving future blinds us to the immediate, actionable insights that are far more useful?

Consider the entrepreneur who spends months perfecting a business plan for an idea that has yet to be tested, or the writer who outlines an entire novel before writing a single chapter. The desire for absolute clarity up front is understandable; it feels safe, responsible. We believe it prevents missteps. Yet, in many complex systems—be they biological, economic, or personal—the most profound understanding doesn't arrive as a flash of complete insight, but rather as an emergent property of iterative action and adjustment. The tension, then, lies between the compelling allure of a perfectly charted course and the messy, uncertain reality that often demands we simply take the next plausible step, even if the destination remains hazy.

## The Argument

True clarity, especially in dynamic environments, is not a static endpoint but an emergent phenomenon, best found by exploring the `adjacent possible`. This concept, coined by Stuart Kauffman, describes the space of all things that are one step away from what currently exists. It suggests that innovation and growth don't leap to radically new states but rather unfold through a series of small, connected transformations. For instance, the invention of the automobile wasn't a sudden departure from horse-drawn carriages to modern cars; it moved through incremental steps like motorizing existing carriage designs. Each step opened up new possibilities that weren't visible from the starting point.

Applied to personal clarity, this means focusing on the next logical, achievable step rather than obsessing over a five-year plan that will almost certainly be rendered obsolete by unforeseen variables. Imagine a career change: instead of trying to perfectly define your dream job five years out, the adjacent possible suggests exploring a skill that complements your current role, networking with someone in an adjacent field, or taking a short course that introduces you to a new domain. Each of these actions provides new information, refines your understanding, and reveals further adjacent possibilities that were previously invisible. This iterative exploration cuts through the noise of overwhelming long-term planning, grounding clarity in observable outcomes and immediate feedback.

The benefit of this approach is twofold: it reduces the paralysis of choice by limiting the scope of immediate action, and it builds resilience by fostering a mindset of continuous learning and adaptation. When you're constantly testing the adjacent possible, you're less invested in a single, rigid vision and more open to the unexpected paths that emerge. Clarity, then, becomes a function of movement, not stasis. It's like navigating through a dense fog: you don't wait for the entire landscape to clear before taking a step; you move carefully, trusting that with each step, more of the path ahead will reveal itself. This process, though seemingly less 'clear' at the outset, leads to a more robust, adaptable, and ultimately clearer understanding of what truly matters as you progress.

## The Counterpoint

A thoughtful skeptic might argue that relying solely on the adjacent possible risks a lack of direction, leaving one adrift in a sea of incremental actions without a guiding star. If you're only ever looking at the next step, how do you ensure you're heading in the right general direction? This approach could be seen as a sophisticated form of procrastination, avoiding the hard work of strategic thinking by perpetually deferring a clear, long-term vision. Without a defined destination, every adjacent step, no matter how well-executed, might simply lead you further down a path that ultimately doesn't align with your deepest aspirations. "Clarity of the adjacent possible" could become an excuse for not committing to a bolder, more intentional future, leading to a fragmented existence rather than a coherent one.

This objection holds significant weight, and it's true that an exclusive focus on the adjacent possible without any larger context can lead to drift. What survives the objection, however, is the idea that the *definition* of clarity itself needs to be flexible. We're not advocating for a complete abandonment of vision, but rather a re-prioritization. The initial 'guiding star' doesn't need to be a perfectly detailed map, but a compass setting. Conceding that some level of directional awareness is necessary, the adjacent possible then becomes the *method* for moving towards that general direction, allowing the path to refine itself as you gain experience and gather new information. It's about recognizing that the best way to clarify the destination is often by starting the journey, not by endlessly planning it from a distance.

## What To Do With It

Instead of trying to define your ultimate, perfect clarity, identify one or two "adjacent experiments" you could run this week. These are small, low-risk actions that will provide new information about your current direction. If you're wrestling with a career decision, perhaps it's a 30-minute informational interview with someone in a field you're curious about, or spending an hour learning a new software tool remotely related to a potential interest. If you're trying to clarify a personal goal, it might be dedicating a small, focused block of time to a neglected hobby, or having a deliberate conversation with a trusted friend about a specific tension you're feeling. The key is to choose an experiment that generates tangible feedback, not just more internal deliberation. Treat these not as steps toward a predetermined outcome, but as probes into the fog, each designed to reveal a little more of the landscape. Pay close attention to the insights and feelings that arise from these small actions, as they are your most reliable indicators of genuine clarity.

*What small, next step could reveal a clearer path forward?*
