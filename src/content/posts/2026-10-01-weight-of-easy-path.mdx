## The Question

We live in an era obsessed with ease. From one-click purchasing to instant information, from automated investments to frictionless social interactions, every facet of modern life is engineered to reduce resistance. The promise is alluring: more time, less stress, greater efficiency. We’re told that friction is a bug, a barrier to be eliminated. But what if this relentless pursuit of ease carries a hidden cost? What if the very things we strive to remove—the delays, the effort, the awkwardness—are precisely what lend weight, meaning, and depth to our experiences and achievements? A smart person could argue that optimizing for ease is simply rational, a liberation from unnecessary drudgery. Why suffer if you don't have to? Yet, another might contend that without some measure of struggle, without the very "friction" we seek to remove, life's rewards feel hollow, our skills remain undeveloped, and our connections superficial. This essay explores the tension between the undeniable appeal of a frictionless existence and the quiet, often unacknowledged, value of useful resistance.

## The Argument

The fundamental premise is that **useful friction builds capacity, deepens experience, and clarifies value**. When things are too easy, we often fail to develop the necessary muscles—both literal and metaphorical—to truly appreciate or sustain our gains. Consider the concept of "desirable difficulty" in learning. Research by Robert Bjork and others has shown that introducing certain obstacles during the learning process, like spacing out study sessions or varying practice conditions, actually leads to stronger, more durable memory and understanding. The initial friction of recall or adaptation strengthens the neural pathways, making the knowledge more deeply embedded and accessible. Without this friction, learning can feel superficial and fleeting.

Beyond learning, useful friction is integral to discipline and mastery. The chef who insists on hand-grinding spices, the writer who drafts in longhand, the musician who practices scales for hours—these are not acts of masochism, but deliberate engagements with resistance. Each act of sustained effort, each small overcoming of inertia, hones their craft and deepens their connection to the work. When every step is automated or simplified, the opportunity for this kind of intimate engagement diminishes. We become passive consumers rather than active creators. The friction of focused attention, of grappling with a difficult problem without immediate algorithmic solutions, is precisely what builds intellectual endurance and genuine problem-solving ability. The value we place on something is often directly proportional to the effort invested. An achievement won easily might be celebrated briefly, but one earned through struggle is cherished and remembered.

## The Counterpoint

The most compelling objection to the value of friction is that it often represents inefficiency and wasted energy. Why romanticize struggle when technology offers elegant solutions? Critics would argue that many forms of friction are indeed mere obstacles: bureaucratic hurdles, tedious manual labor, or redundant processes that drain our time and cognitive resources without adding any real value. To intentionally seek out or maintain such friction would be a form of self-sabotage, preventing us from pursuing higher-order tasks or enjoying leisure. Removing friction, in this view, frees up capacity for innovation, creativity, and deeper human connection. For instance, removing the friction of administrative tasks allows a doctor to spend more time with patients, or an artist to focus solely on their craft. The goal isn't to avoid all challenge, but to intelligently differentiate between *useful* friction that builds character and *unnecessary* friction that merely frustrates.

This is a valid and powerful objection. It is true that not all friction is created equal. There's a clear distinction between the friction of deliberate practice and the friction of a poorly designed system. My argument doesn't suggest a return to an anachronistic, inefficient existence. Instead, it asks us to be more discerning. While we should absolutely strive to remove meaningless administrative friction, we must simultaneously recognize and *protect* the friction that fosters growth, deepens skill, and imbues our efforts with meaning. The concession is that our default should indeed be to question friction, but our ultimate decision should be to selectively retain or even introduce it where it serves a higher purpose, rather than eliminating it wholesale.

## What To Do With It

This week, identify one area where you've been unconsciously optimizing for ease, and deliberately reintroduce a small, useful amount of friction. Perhaps it's responding to emails only at specific times instead of instantly, forcing a delay that encourages more thoughtful replies. Maybe it's turning off notifications on a particular app for a few hours, creating a space for uninterrupted focus. Or it could be committing to a slightly more laborious way of preparing a meal, engaging more deeply with the process rather than seeking the fastest shortcut. The goal isn't to make life harder for its own sake, but to create a small, intentional space where effort is required and, in turn, value is felt more acutely. Observe what changes in your attention, your appreciation, and your sense of accomplishment.

*What essential weight might you be inadvertently shedding in your pursuit of ease?*
